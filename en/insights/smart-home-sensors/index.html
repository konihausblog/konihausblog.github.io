Smart Home Sensors: The Five Families That Tell Your Home What Is Going On

October 31, 2026   ·   7 min read   ·   by Koni
Share

A smart home is only as smart as what it can sense. Lights and blinds are the visible part. Sensors are the quiet part: small devices that tell your home whether a door is open, whether the room is damp, or whether somebody is in the hallway. Without them, automation is just a timer.

This is the third post in the series on the parts of a smart home, after smart lights and smart blinds. Same questions as before: what does it do for you, what should you check before buying, and where are the honest limits.

The five families

1. Motion and presence. A motion sensor notices movement. That is all it knows. Its weakness is simple: if you sit still on the sofa reading, the light goes off, because from the sensor's point of view nothing is happening. A presence sensor adds a small radar (mmWave) to the usual infrared eye, so it can also tell that somebody is in the room even when they hardly move. There is no camera and no picture, just a yes or no.

2. Door and window contacts. Two small pieces, one on the frame and one on the door or window. They answer one question: open or closed? That is the basis for "is the window still open?", for heating that pauses while you air the room, and for a message when the holiday home's cellar door stays open.

3. Temperature and humidity. The quietest and often the most useful. It tells you the real temperature in the rooms that matter, and it shows damp before you see mould.

4. CO2 and air quality. CO2 is a good proxy for "this room needs fresh air". A bedroom with the door closed gets stuffy overnight, and a CO2 sensor shows it in numbers.

5. Water leak. A small puck on the floor under the washing machine, the dishwasher or the water heater. It does nothing until the day it matters.

What you can buy in Switzerland

Prices as of 29 October 2026. They change and stock varies, so treat them as a guide.

Aqara has the broadest range of small sensors. The Presence Multi-Sensor FP300 combines infrared and mmWave radar (6 m range, 120° angle) and also measures light, temperature and humidity. It costs CHF 37.50 at Digitec when in stock, and CHF 64.93 from other sellers. It speaks Thread, Zigbee and Bluetooth and runs on two CR2450 coin cells. Aqara's Temperature and Humidity Sensor T1 uses Zigbee 3.0 and starts at CHF 16.90 on Digitec, with a stated tolerance of 0.6 °C. The Door and Window Sensor is Zigbee as well, with a button cell that Aqara says lasts over two years. Availability varies, so check the price before you plan around it. Aqara states that its sensors need one of its hubs to connect to its own app.

IKEA sells three small sensors for its DIRIGERA hub: VALLHORN (motion), PARASOLL (door and window) and BADRING (water leak). They launched at CHF 12.95, CHF 14.95 and CHF 12.95, so check the current price. IKEA's ALPSTUGA air quality sensor (CHF 34.95) measures CO2, particles and temperature and uses Matter over Thread, so it needs a Thread border router such as the DIRIGERA. Two honest notes: the USB power adapter is sold separately, and reviewers say its display can't be switched off and can be bright in a bedroom.

One thing to check before anything else: how it reports

As with lights and blinds, the brand matters less than the language the sensor speaks.

  • Zigbee and Thread are the ones I look for. They are low-power radios that work locally, without the internet, and they run for years on a small battery.
  • Wi-Fi sensors drain batteries faster and usually need the manufacturer's cloud.
  • A sensor that needs its own hub is fine, as long as that hub can also pass the data to your home system. Otherwise you end up with one app per sensor brand.

Two basics of Zigbee are worth knowing. Sensors on batteries only talk and sleep, so they don't relay other devices' messages. Mains-powered devices, like many smart plugs and bulbs, do relay. That is why a mix of the two makes the mesh reliable: sensors far from the hub depend on powered devices nearby. I wrote about why an open standard matters in no vendor lock-in.

Where I would place the first sensors

Don't buy one of each. Start with the two or three that answer a question you already have.

  1. Water leak sensor under the washing machine or dishwasher. Cheap, and the day it matters it is worth more than anything else on this list.
  2. A temperature and humidity sensor in the room that worries you. A cellar, a holiday home, a bathroom, a bedroom with a damp corner. In the holiday home frost watch, this is the sensor that does the work.
  3. A presence sensor in the hallway. It switches the night light on the way to the bathroom, as in safer hallways at night, and it can switch off the lights in an empty room, where a plain motion sensor would turn them off on you while you read.

Door and window contacts come next, once you know which doors and windows you really care about.

Honest limits

Batteries die quietly. A sensor that has stopped reporting looks the same as a sensor that sees nothing. A good system watches for "last seen" and low-battery warnings and tells you before a sensor goes silent. A door contact that Aqara rates at over two years is not a promise. Mark the date you fitted each battery.

One sensor, one view. A motion sensor can't see a person sitting still. A door contact doesn't know a door is ajar, not open. A temperature sensor on a sunny window sill reads high. Good rules combine two signals instead of trusting one.

Not a safety device. A leak sensor tells you about water. It does not stop it. And none of these replaces a smoke or CO detector, which has to work on its own. I'll come back to that in this series.

How I'd start

  1. Write down the question first. "Did I leave the window open?" "Is the cellar damp?" "Is there water under the machine?"
  2. Pick sensors that speak Zigbee or Thread.
  3. Place them, then watch the numbers for a week before you build any rules on them.
  4. Add the battery check. Every sensor, from day one.
